III / Origins

It was a weapon before it was a ride

The word arrives from the Italian carosello, by way of the French carrousel — the name of a seventeenth-century cavalry tournament, all formation riding and display.

The machine came out of the practice yard. To drill a rider at speed you need speed, so somebody hung wooden horses from a rotating arm and turned it. A cadet took a lance, leaned out, and tried to catch a suspended ring as the ground swung past. Miss, and you came round again. That was the entire point: you always came round again.

The ride kept the geometry and dropped the lance. Everything else on the platform — the cresting, the mirrors, the organ, the horses with their manes thrown back — was added later, to a thing that already worked.

The pleasure came last. First there was only practice.

IV / Fragment

Hold it still and it is a drawing.

Move, and it comes apart into the tiles it was always made of — then finds its way back, because that is the only thing a carousel knows how to do.

V / The ring

Win a prize

The lance became an arm. On the old American machines a dispenser leaned out over the platform and fed out iron rings, one per pass, close enough to snatch at. Somewhere in the batch was a brass one, and the brass one bought you another ride.

So the cavalry game survived intact inside the amusement — same reach, same ring, same reward for timing. Only the stakes had softened.

The booths that grew up around the platform are the game again, standing still. Hoops, a counter, a shelf of prizes, and the same promise the carousel makes: that this time, on this pass, you might catch it.

VI / Framed

A machine built to go nowhere

Every other machine of the nineteenth century was an argument about distance. The carousel refused. Its entire engineering — the crown gear, the cranked rods, the horses rising against their poles — is devoted to bringing a rider back to the exact point they left.

The drawing understands this before we do. The artist never closes a contour. Each horse is a set of open lines that agree, for a moment, to be a horse, and then let go.

VII / Field notes

Six things about the machine

  1. 01

    Direction

    British carousels generally turn clockwise. American ones generally turn counterclockwise, which is why the brass-ring arm hangs on the right.

  2. 02

    The gallop

    Horses did not always rise. Frederick Savage, an engineer in King's Lynn, worked out the overhead cranks in the 1870s that gave them their fall and lift.

  3. 03

    The romance side

    Only the outward-facing flank of each horse is carved in full. The crowd never sees the other side, so the other side was never finished.

  4. 04

    Jumpers and standers

    The outer row often stands still — the largest, most ornate animals, fixed, because the outer edge is where the ring is caught.

  5. 05

    The organ

    A band organ, not a recording. Pipes, drums and a cardboard book of folded music, loud enough to be heard across a field.

  6. 06

    The golden age

    American carving peaked between the 1880s and the Depression. The workshops closed; the horses kept turning.
